You can make jerky in a freeze dryer, and it works best when you treat the machine as a dryer rather than a safety step. A freeze dryer jerky batch goes from raw, marinated strips to shelf-stable in roughly 10 to 14 hours. The meat comes out crisp and brittle instead of chewy, because freeze drying skips the heat that builds chew.

Below I cover what you need, the step-by-step process, the safety mistakes that matter most, a cost and time summary, and answers to the questions readers ask most about [How to Freeze Dry Food at Home](https://www.gaiaverity.com/blog/how-to-freeze-dry-food-at-home/).

## What You Need (and What It Costs)

Start with the right cut. Look for beef that is at least 90% lean, and slice it no more than 1/4 inch thick so it dries completely (TheKitchenToday). Fat turns rancid and greasy over a long shelf life, so a fatty cut undercuts the whole point of freeze drying jerky.

You need a freeze dryer, trays, and parchment to line them. Before the machine enters the picture, the trays go in a deep freezer. Partially freeze the meat 1 to 2 hours until firm but not solid, so it slices cleanly. Depending on how strong your deep freezer is, you can freeze the batch for 3 hours or overnight (Freeze Dried Guide).

Cost is where most people stop. Home freeze dryers run into the thousands, and that shapes every decision after it. A water activity meter is the other line item.

For raw meat prep, the USDA compliance guideline lists optional interventions: preheating the meat or poultry strips in the marinade to a minimum internal temperature of 160°F, dipping in 5% acetic acid for 10 minutes, or acidified sodium chlorite (Keeper®) at concentrations between 500 and 1,200 ppm.

## Step-by-Step: Freeze Drying Jerky

### Chill, then slice

Partially freeze the meat for 1 to 2 hours until firm but not solid. A firm block slices cleanly at 1/4 inch or thinner, and thin slices are what let the cycle finish evenly. Go thicker and you risk a dry shell around a wet center. If your freezer runs cold, check at the one hour mark rather than waiting the full two.

### Marinate and cure

Marinate as you normally would, then give the cure time its due. In one study in *Meat Science*, longer curing times increased the moisture content of jerky made using frozen meat. A short cure on frozen beef leaves you with more water to pull out later. Cure in the fridge at 40°F or below.

### Load, pre-freeze, run

Lay strips flat on trays without overlap. Freeze the loaded trays at least 12 hours or overnight, then run a full cycle of roughly 8 to 12 hours. The machine sublimates ice, not liquid water, so starting cold shortens the run.

### Check and finish

Look for pockets of moisture after the first cycle. If you find any, spread the pieces out and run an additional 2 to 4 hours until completely dry. Total raw-to-shelf-stable time can be as little as 10 to 14 hours. Some guides run the machine for a full 24 hours depending on meat thickness and freeze dryer type, and I would trust your own moisture check over any fixed number.

## Mistakes to Avoid

### Treating the freeze dryer as a kill step

This is the mistake that matters most. Purdue's FS-160-W states plainly that freeze drying "isn't a 'kill step.' This means that bacteria or other potential pathogens aren't removed but are preserved in the product. If moisture is added back to the food, the microorganisms can begin to grow."

So the dryer is preservation, not safety. The kill step happens before the trays.

### Skipping the lethality treatment

USDA's Food Safety and Inspection Service (FSIS) recommendation is to heat meat to 160°F and poultry to 165°F before dehydrating (F157). Apply that same logic here: preheat the strips, then dry them.

### Drying slowly under low humidity

FSIS names "the very slow drying process under low humidity conditions (1% Relative Humidity...)" as one suspected cause of the 2003 *Salmonella* Kiambu outbreak, because it "allowed *Salmonella* organisms to dehydrate during drying and become resistant to heat" (F63). Slow, dry air makes pathogens harder to kill, not easier.

### Letting surface prep drift past 30 minutes

FSIS guidance says the color setting plus surface preparation steps should total 30 minutes or less. Longer, and the establishment must support why the lack of humidity won't dry the product before the lethality treatment (F72, F73, F74).

### Two quality traps

- Fatty cuts fight you. Look for beef at least 90% lean (F95).
- Frozen raw beef won't give you a tender result. Jerky made from frozen and frozen-thawed beef showed "lower drying yield and higher shear force than jerky prepared using fresh beef" (F85). Start fresh if you want tender.

### Storing it badly

Skip that and the work is wasted.

## Cost, Time, and Shelf Life Summary

The clock splits into four parts. Partial freezing runs 1 to 2 hours, enough to firm the meat for clean slicing (THEKITCHENTODAY). Tray pre-freeze is 12 hours or overnight. Then the main cycle runs around 8 to 12 hours, with an additional 2 to 4 hours if you find moisture pockets. Total raw-to-shelf-stable can be as little as 10 to 14 hours, against 6 to 8 hours or longer for traditional dehydrating.

![Diagram: Cost, Time, and Shelf Life Summary](/diagrams/napkin-1bc5e8a376.png)

| Stage | Time |
|---|---|
| Partial freeze | 1 to 2 hours |
| Tray pre-freeze | 12 hours or overnight |
| Main cycle | 8 to 12 hours |
| Extra drying if needed | 2 to 4 hours |
| Total | 10 to 14 hours |

Most of that is unattended machine time. Once trays are loaded, you wait.

Sealed properly, freeze dried beef jerky can last up to 25 years when properly sealed and stored. To eat it, soak strips in water 30 to 60 minutes until pliable, or microwave 20 to 30 seconds to soften without overcooking.

My take: the tray pre-freeze is where home cooks cut corners, and it's the wrong corner. Fridge-cold meat isn't frozen meat. Solid trays dry more evenly, and uneven drying is what forces the 2 to 4 hour second pass.

## Freeze Drying vs. Dehydrating, and Whether It's Worth It

Purdue's FS-160-W is blunt about this: for a chewy texture, "jerky is an example," dehydration is superior to freeze drying. The heat in a dehydrator collapses cell walls and drives off volatile compounds, which costs aroma but builds the chew people expect from jerky. Freeze drying skips that heat, so you get a crisper, more brittle strip that rehydrates fast.

### Time and cost

Dehydrating beef jerky takes 6 to 8 hours or longer (F99). Freeze drying runs 8 to 12 hours for the main cycle, plus a 1 to 2 hour partial freeze and 12 hours or overnight for tray pre-freeze, though some guides run a full 24 hours depending on meat thickness and machine type (F91, F92, F97, F153).

| | Dehydrator | Freeze dryer |
|---|---|---|
| Chew | Yes | No |
| Cycle time | 6 to 8 hours or longer | 8 to 12 hours, plus pre-freeze |
| Shelf life | Months | Up to 25 years, sealed (F107) |
| Equipment | Budget | Thousands |

### My take

If chew is the whole point of jerky for you, I'd skip the freeze dryer and stay with a dehydrator. The common advice says freeze drying is always the upgrade. It isn't, because texture is what jerky gets judged on.

Where that flips: long-term storage. Sealed properly, freeze dried jerky holds up to 25 years (F104). If you're stocking a pantry instead of snacking, the tradeoff makes sense.

## FAQ

### Can you do jerky in a freeze dryer?

Yes. A freeze dryer can run a full jerky cycle, and the kitchentoday guide gives a total of 10 to 14 hours from raw meat to a shelf-stable product, including an 8 to 12 hour main drying cycle. One caution sits at the center of this whole method. Purdue's FS-160-W states plainly that freeze-drying "isn't a 'kill step,'" meaning pathogens survive the cycle and can reactivate if moisture returns. So freeze-drying jerky is a preservation step, not a safety step. For me, the freeze dryer is the wrong tool for jerky, and I'd use a dehydrator instead.

### Is it illegal to sell homemade beef jerky?

Not categorically, but you can't legally sell it from a home kitchen without meeting federal rules. The USDA compliance guideline says jerky producers have to control food safety hazards and document that their HACCP systems work. The FSIS jerky validation report is stricter still, requiring processors to validate a 5-log reduction of E. Coli O157:H7 and a greater than 6.5-log reduction of Salmonella. Home operations rarely hold that documentation. If selling is your goal, check your state rules first.

### Is it better to freeze dry or dehydrate meat?

For jerky specifically, dehydrate. Purdue's FS-160-W makes the case directly, noting that "when a chewy texture is desired, jerky is an example, dehydration is superior to freeze drying." Dehydration also concentrates flavor, while Purdue notes the heat can drive off volatile compounds and leave a less complex flavor profile. Freeze-dried jerky does rehydrate in 30 to 60 minutes of soaking, which suits backpacking. That convenience is real, but texture is the trade. For eating at home, I wouldn't bother with the freeze dryer.

### Can a freeze dryer dehydrate?

No, not the way a dehydrator does. A freeze dryer pulls water out by sublimation, turning ice straight to vapor under vacuum. A dehydrator moves warm air across the food. Different mechanisms, and you can't flip a freeze dryer into a warm-air mode. That matters for jerky because the chew comes from heat-driven dehydration. If you want a dehydrator, buy a dehydrator.

### Freeze dryer for $2700. Who would ever break even?

I'm skeptical anyone breaks even on jerky alone. A Harvest Right unit costs several thousand dollars, and what comes out isn't the chewy product most people want. The math only improves if you run one machine across many foods, since Purdue notes freeze-drying preserves nutrition and color. Cost per jar drops fast when the same unit handles fruit, meals and leftovers. That's the honest case for buying one. If jerky is your only goal, the machine will never pay for itself.

## What Components Do You Need For a DIY Rainwater Harvesting System?

The components of a functional rainwater collection systems diy project range from a simple barrel attached to a downspout to complex cistern setups. I have grouped the necessary items based on the scale of the project you envision.

![Diagram: What Components Do You Need For a DIY Rainwater Harvesting System?](/diagrams/napkin-743c488bd0.png)

For the smallest setups, like a basic rain barrel, you will need a food-grade container, such as a 50-gallon sealed barrel, and the necessary plumbing to attach it to a downspout. For larger needs, you will require more substantial components. A full rainwater system needs a few key elements:

* **Catchment Surface:** This is typically your roof, which must be sealed using products that are free of lead and fixtures that are non-corrosive, according to the Rainwater Harvesting Design Specification.
* **Conveyance:** This involves gutters and downspouts to move the runoff.
* **Pre-Filtration:** Leaf screens are needed to catch large debris. A first flush diverter is also important, as one rule of thumb for first-flush diversion is to divert a minimum of 10 gallons for every 1,000 square feet of collection surface for every 1 inch of rainfall, according to the OSU Extension Service.
* **Storage:** This is either a small rain barrel, which holds about 55 to 60 gallons, or a much larger cistern, which can hold 200 to 1,000+ gallons.
* **Delivery System:** This can be a gravity feed or a pumped system. If you opt for a pump, you will need to consider its power source.

Regarding costs, I can't provide specific price points, but I can tell you that for a single barrel setup, the cost of the tank and associated supplies is in the budget range, while larger cistern installations involve a more significant upfront investment.

## Step-by-Step Guide to Setting Up a Rain Collection System DIY

The process of how to make your own rainwater collection system varies based on whether you are building a simple barrel setup or a large cistern. I suggest I start with the calculation phase before moving to assembly.

Start by figuring out how much water you actually need.

Then work out what you can collect. The Department of Energy gives this formula: Monthly Rainfall Collected (gal) = Catchment Area (size in square feet) × Monthly Rainwater Harvesting Potential (inches) × Collection Efficiency × 0.62. Run the numbers on a 1,000 square foot roof with 1 inch of rain and you get roughly 620 gallons, as AgriLife Today at TAMU notes.

Now you know the size. You can build. A simple rain barrel just means connecting the downspout to the barrel. For more advanced systems, the sequence is:

1. Install the catchment surface and gutters, ensuring they can handle a 100-year storm event, as advised by the OSU Extension Service.
2. Place the primary filtration components, including the first flush diverter.
3. Position the storage tank on a stable foundation, remembering that water is heavy at 8 pounds per gallon, meaning a full 3,000-gallon tank weighs over 24,000 pounds, according to the OSU Extension Service.
4. Connect the delivery system, whether gravity-fed or pumped.

I would skip the complex plumbing calculations until after you have determined your required storage volume.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid in Your DIY Rainwater Harvesting System

When attempting a rainwater collection systems diy project, there are several pitfalls I have observed in the literature. The most significant error is underestimating the initial debris load. Many people forget the crucial function of the first flush diverter.

I strongly advise against simply attaching a downspout directly to a storage tank without any preliminary filtering. The debris washed off the roof during the first rain event can clog your entire system. Also, I think it's a mistake to assume a perfect 100% collection efficiency.

Structural capacity is another thing people miss. Go big with a cistern and you're dealing with serious weight. The OSU Extension Service puts a number on it: a full 3,000-gallon tank weighs over 24,000 pounds. Skip that math when you plan the foundation and you've got a real safety problem.

## DIY Rainwater Harvesting System Options

You've got several ways to build a rainwater collection systems diy setup. Which one fits depends on what you need.

### Small Scale: The Basic Rain Barrel

This is the simplest method. Attach a food-grade container, like one of the 50-gallon options, to the downspout. Great for small, immediate garden needs. But the Snohomish County, WA - Official Website points out that a single barrel typically runs dry after a few weeks without rain. You need frequent rain to keep irrigation going.

### Medium Scale: Linked Barrels

Think one barrel won't carry you to the next rain event? Link several smaller ones with PVC or flexible tubing.

### Large Scale: Full Cistern Systems

Substantial irrigation, or a property with real rainfall, calls for a large cistern. These systems need careful planning for ground stability because of their immense weight. The Department of Energy review details sizing the tank against expected demand versus potential harvest.

### Specialized Approach: Pump Integration

If your storage tank sits lower than your garden beds, you'll need a pump. External on-demand pumps come in 110/120 Volt, 12 Volt, or solar powered configurations, according to the homeowners guide from swfwmd.state.fl.us.

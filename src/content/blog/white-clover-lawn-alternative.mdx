## The short answer

A white clover lawn is either pure clover or, more often, clover mixed into grass. It needs less water and fertilizer than a grass lawn and feeds bees. It also wears faster, needs reseeding, and rules out most weed killers.

Before you buy seed, make three choices:

1. **Pure clover or a clover and grass mix.** A mix is tougher.
2. **Dutch white clover or microclover.** Dutch is cheap and vigorous. Micro is shorter and tidier.
3. **Fall or spring planting.** Fall is usually better.

If you're still deciding whether clover is right for your yard at all, start with our [clover lawn pros and cons](/blog/clover-lawn/). This page is for when you've decided and want to do it well.

## Clover used to be in every lawn

For most of the last century, clover wasn't a weed. It was a selling point. University of Maryland Extension notes that before broadleaf herbicides arrived in the 1950s, clover was a valued part of lawn seed mixes. It fed the grass by pulling nitrogen from the air, and it kept lawns green.

Then came herbicides that killed every broadleaf plant in the lawn, clover included. Over time, clover went from a feature to something people sprayed. Planting it now is a return to how lawns used to work.

## Pure clover or mixed with grass?

**A mix is the safer bet for most yards.**

The best recent evidence comes from turf research at Utah State, shared by the University of Minnesota. Researchers grew four clovers (white, strawberry, crimson, and rose) mixed with Kentucky bluegrass. All four lasted through the season. Every mix looked as good as or better than bluegrass alone, whether or not it got fertilizer.

Pure clover has real drawbacks:

- **It's less durable.** The Spruce, written by a Penn State Master Gardener, notes clover alone doesn't hold up to foot traffic like turf. Mixed with grass, it handles traffic much better.
- **It looks rough off-season.** Maryland Extension says microclover's top growth dies back in winter, which can leave bare spots.
- **It can struggle in summer heat.** Maryland found microclover starts dying in hot, dry weather and needs reseeding to persist there.

Maryland Extension goes further. It says there is no current research supporting microclover alone as a lawn alternative in Maryland. In the cooler, wetter Pacific Northwest, Oregon State has had better results with mixed "ecolawns" of clover, low grasses, and other flowering plants.

**Pure clover can work** for low-traffic areas, slopes, and spots where grass has always struggled. For a yard that kids and dogs run on, mix it.

## Choosing your seed

| | Dutch white clover | Microclover |
|---|---|---|
| Height | About 4 to 6 inches (NC State) | Shorter, with smaller leaves |
| Flowers | Lots, which is great for bees | Fewer |
| Spread | Aggressive, roots as it creeps | Less aggressive, more clumping |
| Look in a lawn | Coarser and more obvious | Blends in with grass |
| Cost | Cheap and easy to find | Pricier and harder to find in stores (Maryland) |

Microclover isn't a separate species. Maryland Extension explains it's a group of smaller white clover varieties, such as 'Pirouette' and 'Pipolina'.

**Hardiness:** NC State lists white clover for USDA zones 3 to 10. That covers most of the continental US.

**What to look for on the bag:**

- **Inoculated or coated seed.** Clover fixes nitrogen with help from soil bacteria. Pre-inoculated seed carries those bacteria, which helps new lawns where clover hasn't grown before.
- **A recent test date and high germination rate** on the seed tag.
- **No weed seed** listed, or very little.

## How to plant a white clover lawn

### When to plant

Timing depends on where you live:

- **Cold-winter regions:** University of Minnesota Extension recommends late-fall "dormant" seeding, after it's too cold for seeds to sprout. They sit in the soil over winter and come up in spring. Spring seeding is their second choice.
- **California and mild-winter regions:** UC Davis favors fall planting, with water during early-fall and late-spring sowings.
- **Everywhere:** Avoid seeding right before a hot, dry stretch.

### How much seed

**Adding clover to an existing lawn:** University of Minnesota Extension recommends about **1.1 ounces of Dutch white clover per 1,000 square feet** as part of a mixed lawn. Clover seed is tiny, so a little goes far.

**Pure clover lawns:** We couldn't find an extension service that gives a standard rate. Seed companies' recommendations vary a lot by product and coating. Follow the rate on your bag, and lean toward the higher end for bare soil.

### Steps

1. **Mow existing grass short** and rake out thatch so seed can reach the soil. For a new lawn, loosen the top inch and rake it smooth.
2. **Mix seed with dry sand** for even spreading. Clover seed is too small to spread evenly on its own.
3. **Spread half the seed walking one direction and half walking across it.**
4. **Press it in.** Walk on it, roll it, or rake lightly. Seed needs to touch soil. Don't bury it deep.
5. **Keep it moist.** Oregon State says regular watering is necessary through germination and establishment, especially the first summer. Light, frequent watering beats one heavy soak while seeds sprout.

## What happens in years one through five

Most guides stop at planting. Here's what to expect after.

**Year one:** Slow at first, then filling in. Keep watering through the first summer. Mow high if you mow at all, so the clover isn't shaded by grass.

**Year two:** This is when clover usually looks its best. It spreads by creeping stems that root as they go, so gaps close on their own. In a mix, you'll see it settle into patches where the grass is weakest.

**Years three to five:** Expect thinning. UC Davis notes that white clover's main stem seldom lasts more than two years. The patch survives because new plants keep rooting and reseeding, not because the original plants live long. Some spots will thin out, especially after hot summers or hard winters.

**Ongoing care:**

- **Overseed thin spots** each fall or spring. Treat it as routine.
- **Let some flowers set seed** before mowing in summer, so the lawn partly reseeds itself.
- **Skip broadleaf weed killers.** Maryland Extension warns that most herbicides labeled for lawns will kill clover. That means no weed-and-feed products. Pull weeds by hand or spot-treat carefully.
- **Watch for southern blight** in hot, humid weather. Maryland notes it can occasionally hit microclover.

## Living with a clover lawn

**Bees.** Clover flowers draw a lot of bees. That's the point for many people, and it's a real benefit for pollinators. If someone in your house is allergic to stings, or kids play barefoot, Maryland Extension flags this as a downside. Mowing just before flowers open keeps bee traffic down in play areas.

**Slippery when wet.** Clover leaves are softer and wetter than grass blades. Dense clover can be slick after rain, especially on slopes.

**Shade.** Maryland lists poor shade tolerance as a microclover drawback. NC State rates white clover for sun to part shade. Neither is a deep-shade plant. For shady spots, look at a [moss lawn](/blog/moss-lawn/) instead.

**Mowing.** Oregon State says its ecolawn mixes usually need mowing only every two to three weeks through the growing season. Mowing in late summer, after flowers have dropped seed, helps clover spread.

**Dogs.** Clover is soft underfoot, but pure clover wears down faster than grass where dogs run. A mix holds up better.

## How much water and fertilizer you'll save

The numbers often quoted online are rounded or misread, so here's what the extension sources actually say.

**Water:** Oregon State estimates that established ecolawns need **one-quarter to one-third as much water** as a conventional lawn. That's for clover mixed with low grasses and other broadleaf plants, and only after establishment. The first summer still needs regular watering.

**Fertilizer:** In Maryland's microclover and tall fescue research, typical tall fescue lawns need 2 to 3 pounds of nitrogen per 1,000 square feet a year. With microclover in the mix, the anticipated need dropped to 1 to 2 pounds. Maryland Extension summarizes that as possibly cutting **one nitrogen application per year**.

Those are real savings. They're also smaller than "cut maintenance by 70%," and they depend on a healthy, established stand.

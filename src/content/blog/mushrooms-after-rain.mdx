## The short answer

The fungus was already in your soil. Rain gives it the water it needs to push up mushrooms, which are its way of releasing spores. Most lawn mushrooms are harmless to grass and a sign of soil with plenty of organic matter. Don't eat them, keep dogs and small kids away, and pick them by hand if they bother you.

That's the whole story for most yards. The rest of this page is for when you want to know how fast they come, which ones matter, and when they point to a real problem.

## Why rain brings mushrooms up

A mushroom is only the fruit. The living fungus is a web of fine threads called mycelium, spread through the top few inches of soil. It feeds on dead things: old roots, buried wood, thatch, leaves.

Mycelium can sit there for months doing quiet work. When the soil gets wet enough, it builds mushrooms fast. Water swells the cells, the cap pushes up, and it starts dropping spores. That's why a lawn can look clean in the evening and dotted with caps by morning. The ASPCA notes that mushrooms can appear overnight during rainy stretches.

Rain alone isn't the full trigger. Temperature matters too. You'll see that in the Seattle numbers below.

## How long after rain? What three years of data show

Most pages say mushrooms show up "a few days" after rain. We wanted to check that against real sightings.

We pulled every verified iNaturalist sighting of eight common lawn mushrooms within 60 km of three cities from 2023 through 2025. Then we matched them to NOAA daily rainfall at each city's main airport. That's 2,895 lawn mushroom sightings in all.

![Bar chart showing lawn mushroom sightings correlate most with the previous week's rain in Dallas and Atlanta](/mushrooms-after-rain-lag.png)

Here's what showed up:

- **In Dallas and Atlanta, sightings lined up best with the previous week's rain.** The correlation was 0.35 in Dallas and 0.31 in Atlanta. Rain that same week mattered less.
- **By the second week, the link was mostly gone.** Mushrooms come up, drop their spores, and collapse within days.
- **Seattle was different.** Its link to weekly rain was weaker, because Seattle's story is seasonal.

These are modest correlations, not a stopwatch. iNaturalist counts reflect when people are out looking, not only when mushrooms are up. But the pattern held in two very different climates: rain this week, mushrooms next week.

### Seattle: the fall rains are the trigger

In the Pacific Northwest, the big flush comes when the dry summer ends.

![Chart of Seattle monthly rainfall and fungi sightings, with sightings peaking in October and November](/mushrooms-after-rain-seattle.png)

Across all fungi around Seattle, July averaged about 900 sightings. October and November each averaged more than 10,000. That's more than 11 times as many.

December is the wettest month, though, and sightings drop by more than half. Cold soil slows the fungus down. Wet plus mild is the combination that matters.

## What's in your lawn after rain

These are the lawn species we tracked, plus what to know about each. Use this as a starting point, not as permission to eat anything.

| Mushroom | What it looks like | Should you worry? |
|---|---|---|
| Green-spored parasol (*Chlorophyllum molybdites*) | Large white cap with brown scales. Gills start white and turn greenish. Often in rings or arcs in warm states | Yes. Poisonous. Causes severe vomiting and diarrhea |
| Meadow mushroom (*Agaricus campestris*) | White cap, gills pink at first, then chocolate brown | Edible for experts only. Easy to confuse with toxic lookalikes |
| Mower's mushroom (*Panaeolus foenisecii*) | Small brown cap, dark brown gills, common right after mowing | Don't eat. Not established as safe |
| Shaggy mane (*Coprinus comatus*) | Tall white shaggy cylinder that melts into black ink | Edible for experts. Dissolves within hours of picking |
| Yellow fieldcap (*Bolbitius titubans*) | Small, slimy, egg-yolk yellow cap that fades fast | Don't eat. Collapses within a day |
| White dunce cap (*Conocybe apala*) | Tiny cone-shaped pale cap on a thin, fragile stem | Don't eat. Some close relatives are deadly |
| Fairy ring mushroom (*Marasmius oreades*) | Tan cap with a bump, tough bendy stem, grows in rings | Edible for experts. Has a toxic lookalike. See [fairy ring mushrooms](/blog/fairy-ring-mushrooms/) |
| Puffballs (*Calvatia* and others) | Round white balls with no gills | Only young ones that are pure white inside are edible. Some deadly mushrooms look like puffballs before they open |

The green-spored parasol deserves extra attention. Oklahoma State plant pathologist Steve Marek told KFOR it's likely the white lawn mushroom most Oklahomans find after rain. He also said it can stay in a yard for about a week.

For deeper help with identification, see our guides to [common lawn mushroom types](/blog/common-lawn-mushroom-types/) and [white mushrooms in your lawn](/blog/white-mushrooms-in-lawn/).

## Are they dangerous to kids and dogs?

Some are. University of California IPM puts it plainly: there's no simple test that tells a safe mushroom from a poisonous one. So the rule at home is easy. Nobody eats lawn mushrooms.

**If a child eats one:** Call Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222. Save a mushroom, or take a photo of it from the top, side, and underneath. That helps the experts a lot.

**If a dog eats one:** Call your vet or the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at **(888) 426-4435**. The ASPCA says wild mushrooms can cause stomach upset, neurological signs, or liver damage that can be fatal. If your dog is already vomiting, drooling, stumbling, or acting strange, go straight to a vet.

Dogs are the bigger day-to-day risk because they eat first and ask later. A quick walk around the yard after a rainy week, before you let them out, does a lot. We cover this in more detail in [lawn mushrooms and dogs](/blog/lawn-mushrooms-and-dogs/) and [are lawn mushrooms poisonous?](/blog/are-lawn-mushrooms-poisonous/)

## Do mushrooms hurt your lawn?

No. Lawn mushrooms feed on dead organic matter, not living grass. UC Agriculture and Natural Resources says a few mushrooms can be a good sign. It means your soil has plenty of organic matter and active life in it.

A lot of mushrooms is different. It can mean the lawn is too wet, drains poorly, or gets too much shade. That's useful information, not an emergency.

## What to do, and what not to do

**Do:**

- **Pick them by hand.** Wear gloves if you like. Pick them before the caps fully open and drop spores. Compost them or put them in yard waste.
- **Rake up extra debris.** Heavy thatch, buried wood chips, and leftover roots are food for the fungus.
- **Cut back watering in rainy weeks.** If it rained, your lawn doesn't need irrigation too.
- **Fix drainage where water pools.** Leveling low spots or core aeration helps compacted soil drain.
- **Let in more light** if shade is a problem. Trimming low branches dries the surface faster.

**Don't:**

- **Don't mow over them.** UC ANR advises against it, because mowing breaks them up and spreads the fungus.
- **Don't spray fungicide.** Few fungicides sold to homeowners work on lawn mushrooms, and they also kill the helpful fungi in your soil. Wisconsin Extension says products labeled for fairy rings usually don't work either.
- **Don't eat them**, no matter how much one looks like the store kind.

Most flushes fade on their own within a few days once the soil dries.

## When it's a sign of something bigger

Mushrooms after rain are normal. These patterns are worth a closer look:

- **The same spot every time it rains.** There's probably buried wood there, like an old stump or construction debris. Mushrooms will keep coming until it breaks down.
- **Mushrooms on dry weeks too.** Check for a leaking irrigation line or a spot that never drains.
- **A ring of dead or dark green grass.** That's likely a fairy ring. Our [fairy ring guide](/blog/fairy-ring-mushrooms/) covers what to do.
- **Mushrooms at the base of a tree or on its roots.** Some of these fungi decay wood in living trees. Have an arborist take a look.

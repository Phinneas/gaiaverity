## The short answer

Most tomato companion advice is repeated from one garden blog to the next without anyone checking it. We looked at field and greenhouse studies for 14 common pairings.

The best-supported picks are **onions** and **basil** for pests, **lettuce** to use the space under the vines, and **marigolds** if you plan ahead. The biggest real warning is **corn**. Several famous "never plant near tomatoes" rules turn out to be weak.

## How we graded each pairing

We searched peer-reviewed trials and university extension publications for each claim. Then we gave it one of three grades:

- **Supported:** A controlled study on tomatoes shows the effect.
- **Mixed:** Some studies show a benefit, others don't, or it only works under specific conditions.
- **Folklore:** We found no tomato study that tests it.

"Folklore" doesn't mean a pairing is bad. It means nobody has shown the claimed benefit with tomatoes yet.

## Best companions by job

### For pest control: onions and basil

**Onions** have the strongest numbers we found. In a controlled field trial, tomatoes interplanted with onions had 63% fewer aphids, 56% fewer whiteflies, and 25% fewer thrips than tomatoes grown alone.

**Chives** reduced tomato leafminer egg laying and leaf damage in cage and screenhouse tests. The researchers still want open-field trials before calling it proven.

**Garlic** is the cautionary one. In a two-season Egyptian field study, it cut leafminers but increased thrips and spider mites. Companion effects are pest-specific, not a general shield.

**Basil** reduced whiteflies by about 15 to 18% in a Kenyan field study. It worked better paired with insect netting. It didn't increase yield on its own in a three-year greenhouse trial.

### For whiteflies in a greenhouse: French marigolds

A 2019 greenhouse study found French marigolds (*Tagetes patula*) kept glasshouse whiteflies off tomatoes, likely through the limonene they release. The catch is timing. Marigolds planted from the start helped. Marigolds added after a heavy infestation did little.

### For space: lettuce

This is the best-supported pairing on the list. Four greenhouse experiments in Brazil found that lettuce planted between tomatoes didn't reduce tomato yield or fruit grade. You get a second crop from the same bed.

One caveat: as tomatoes grow, they shade the lettuce hard. A Chilean study recorded lettuce yields about 75% lower under tomatoes than in open beds. Plant lettuce the same day as your tomato transplants, or slightly before, and harvest it before the vines close in.

### For pollinators: borage

Borage is a bee magnet. In a Polish field study, bees made up 73% of the insects visiting its flowers. That's good for the garden overall. Tomatoes mostly pollinate themselves, so don't expect a big tomato yield jump from it.

## What not to plant with tomatoes

### Corn after it silks

This is the warning with the best evidence. The corn earworm and the tomato fruitworm are the same moth, *Helicoverpa zea*. Utah State University Extension advises keeping tomatoes away from corn once the silks turn brown, because moths then leave the corn looking for new hosts.

There's a twist. A 2014 field study used fresh corn as a trap crop and found 2 to 3 times as many earworm eggs on tomatoes *without* a corn border. Timing decides whether corn protects or threatens your tomatoes. For most home gardens, simple distance is the safer bet.

### Potatoes, if blight is common where you live

Tomatoes and potatoes share late blight and the Colorado potato beetle. Late blight spreads in cool, wet weather. If your area gets it, keep the two crops apart and don't plant one where the other grew last year. No study has measured exactly how much distance helps, though.

### The warnings that don't hold up

**Fennel.** Almost every list says fennel stunts tomatoes. An 11-week greenhouse study growing them together found no evidence of that. Ask Extension also judged the research too thin to call fennel harmful to transplants.

**Cabbage and broccoli.** We found no study showing they hurt tomatoes. A cabbage and tomato field trial recorded tomato yields slightly above tomatoes grown alone. Texas A&M AgriLife lists broccoli as a good tomato companion.

**Black walnut.** This one is more nuanced. Pure juglone stunted tomatoes in a lab at high doses and actually helped growth at low doses. Washington State University Extension cautions that lab results don't prove what happens near a real tree. Many gardeners do report trouble under walnuts, so skip the risk if you can.

## Marigolds and nematodes: the real story

This is the most repeated claim in tomato gardening, so it gets its own section.

Marigolds can suppress root-knot nematodes. But the University of Florida IFAS says the reliable method is to **grow marigolds as a cover crop for at least two months before planting tomatoes**, then remove them. Interplanting them with tomatoes is far less dependable.

In a California field study, tomato yields after a marigold cover crop were about 50% higher than after bare fallow. That was the before-planting method, not side by side. The variety matters too. Some marigold cultivars suppressed nematodes and some didn't.

If nematodes are your problem, plan marigolds a season ahead.

## Container combinations

Containers squeeze every plant into one pot of soil and one water supply, so pick companions that stay small.

- **5 to 10 gallons:** Tomato plus one basil or a few chives.
- **15 to 20 gallons:** Tomato plus basil plus a French marigold, or tomato plus a ring of lettuce.
- **25 gallons or more:** Tomato, basil, marigold, and lettuce, with the lettuce harvested young.

Use potting mix, not garden soil. Give a tomato about 5 gallons alone and 7 to 10 gallons with one companion. Containers with companions dry out fast in summer, so check them daily. Compact varieties like 'Tiny Tim' or 'Patio Princess' leave room for a neighbor.

## Spacing and layout

**Determinate tomatoes:** 24 to 36 inches apart. **Indeterminate tomatoes:** 36 to 48 inches apart, plus room for the cage or trellis.

Layer companions by height so nothing fights for the same light:

- **Tall:** Tomatoes
- **Medium:** Basil, borage, peppers
- **Low:** Lettuce, onions, chives, marigolds, carrots
- **Edges:** Nasturtiums, thyme, oregano

A good starter bed: tomatoes in the center, a row of onions or chives along the sunny edge, basil between the plants, and lettuce tucked underneath early in the season.

  - question: Can I really use a regular fridge as a root cellar?
    answer: Yes. A spare fridge already delivers the two hardest things to recreate — cold and dark — in an insulated, mouse-proof box. The only real work is fixing the two things it gets wrong. It dehumidifies instead of humidifying, and it runs slightly too cold for some crops.
  - question: What temperature should a root cellar fridge be set to?
    answer: 32–40°F for most root vegetables and apples. Potatoes and winter squash prefer 45–55°F, which is above a standard fridge's range — you need an external temperature controller to hold those warmer settings.
  - question: How do I add humidity to a root cellar fridge?
    answer: Put an open pan of water on the bottom shelf, drape a damp towel, or run a small humidifier inside. Store produce in perforated bags or open bins to keep local humidity up. Aim for 85–95% relative humidity, and watch it with a hygrometer.
  - question: How long does produce last in a root cellar fridge?
    answer: It depends on the crop. Carrots and beets keep 4–6 months, apples 2–6 months, potatoes 4–6 months kept cool, and cabbage 3–4 months. Stored right, a fridge root cellar beats any pantry and matches a basement cold room.
  - question: What should I not store in a root cellar fridge?
    answer: Keep onions and garlic out — they want cool and dry, not humid. Don't pack ripe fruit with root vegetables (ethylene gas makes neighbors spoil), and never store produce you washed, since trapped moisture rots it fast.

A root cellar gives you cold, darkness, and humidity — the three conditions that let root vegetables, apples, and cabbage sit in storage for months instead of spoiling in a pantry. The old way meant digging into a hillside. The shortcut is sitting in your garage right now: an extra refrigerator already has the cold and the dark, and it's insulated and mouse-proof to boot.

What it lacks is humidity, and its thermostat runs a few degrees cold for some crops. Fix those two things and a $50 fridge beats a $5,000 cellar build. Here's how to turn an ordinary fridge into a root cellar refrigerator.

## What a Root Cellar Fridge Needs

The gear list is short. You probably own most of it already.

| Item | What it does | Rough cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Spare fridge (garage, mini, or old unit) | The insulated cold box | $0 if you have one; $100–400 used |
| Thermometer and hygrometer | Tells you the actual temp and humidity inside | $10–15 |
| Temperature controller | Holds warmer temps (45–55°F) the fridge can't reach on its own | $30–45 |
| Water pan, damp towel, or small humidifier | Adds the humidity a fridge strips away | $0–15 |
| Perforated bags, bins, and crates | Breathable storage that keeps humidity near the produce | $10–25 |

## Step-by-Step: Turn a Fridge into a Root Cellar

```napkin
Create a flowchart:
Pick a spare working fridge -> Clean and dry it -> Set to 38-40 F -> Add a water pan for humidity -> Load produce in breathable bins -> Keep apples away from potatoes -> Check temp and humidity daily
```

**1. Choose the right fridge.** A working unit you can spare — a second fridge in the garage, a dorm fridge, or an old one headed for the curb. Place it somewhere that stays above freezing in winter; an unheated shed that hits 10°F will defeat the thermostat.

**2. Clean and dry it completely.** Wash shelves and walls with a mild bleach solution, then leave the door open for a day until it's bone dry. A dirty fridge is a mold starter, and you're about to make it humid on purpose.

**3. Set the temperature.** 38–40°F is the sweet spot for carrots, beets, cabbage, and apples. Let it stabilize for a few hours, then verify with the thermometer rather than trusting the dial.

**4. Add humidity.** Set a shallow pan of water on the bottom shelf. A fridge's compressor pulls moisture out of the air, so you're fighting it constantly — check the pan every few days. If the hygrometer reads below 85%, add a damp towel or a small humidifier.

**5. Load produce the right way.** No airtight plastic tubs and no sealed bags — produce needs airflow, or condensation turns into rot. Use perforated bags, slatted crates, or open bins, and don't pack anything against the back wall where it can freeze.

**6. Keep enemies apart.** Apples and pears put off ethylene gas that makes potatoes sprout and carrots turn bitter. Give them their own shelf or their own bin, ideally with a folded towel between.

**7. Watch it for the first week.** Check temperature and humidity daily until it holds steady, then weekly after that. The first week is when every setup mistake shows up.

## Mistakes to Avoid

**Washing produce before you store it.** Rinsing carrots or beets traps moisture against the skin and invites rot. Brush off the loose soil instead, and wash only when you take them out to cook.

**Setting the fridge too cold.** The dial's "coldest" setting can push the crisper below freezing, which ruptures cell walls and leaves you with mushy, translucent produce. Stay at 38°F and confirm with a thermometer.

**Forgetting humidity.** This is the mistake that ruins most root cellar fridge attempts. A dry fridge shrivels carrots, wilts cabbage, and turns apples into rubber. Without water in there, you've built a dehumidifier, not a cellar.

**Storing apples with potatoes and onions.** Ethylene from the fruit speeds up sprouting and spoilage. This one habit — separating fruit from roots — buys you weeks of extra storage.

**Using airtight containers.** Sealed bins trap the very moisture that then condenses and molds. Produce must breathe.

**Putting the fridge in a freezing space.** A fridge can cool, but it can't heat. In a below-freezing garage or shed, the interior drops with the outside temperature and everything freezes regardless of the dial.

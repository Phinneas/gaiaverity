## The short answer

A fairy ring is one fungus spreading outward from a single starting point in the soil. Mushrooms pop up along the outer edge, so they trace a circle. In most home lawns it's a cosmetic problem, and fungicides rarely fix it. The useful question is which of the three ring types you have, because each one needs something different.

## How a fairy ring forms

It starts with one spore landing on buried food: an old stump, dead roots, leftover lumber, or a thick layer of thatch. The fungus grows threads called mycelium outward in every direction, like ripples in a pond.

As it spreads, the center runs out of food and dies off. The living part becomes a band moving outward. That band is where the mushrooms appear after rain, so you see a ring.

About 60 kinds of fungi can form rings, according to the Foraging Course Company. In North American lawns, the best known is *Marasmius oreades*, the fairy ring mushroom.

### How fast and how old

Rings grow slowly, and the sources don't agree on one number:

- **About 30 cm (1 foot) a year** for *Marasmius oreades*, according to the RHS
- **Up to 20 cm (8 inches) a year** for free-growing rings, according to Utah State University
- **1 to 2 feet a year** for lawn rings in general, according to Nevada's Department of Agriculture

Some rings are very old. A 2023 study of fairy rings in Wyoming's Laramie Basin estimated ages up to 522 years. Most were younger, though, with 90% under 80 years old. Utah State describes a ring in France thought to be about 700 years old and roughly 600 meters across.

Wisconsin Extension says lawn rings can reach 15 feet in diameter. If your ring is that big, it has likely been there for years before you noticed it.

## Which ring do you have?

Wisconsin Extension sorts fairy rings into three types based on what they do to the grass. Match what you see to the type, then follow its fix.

| What you see | Type | What's happening | What to do |
|---|---|---|---|
| A ring of brown or dead grass, often with greener grass just inside or outside | Type 1 | The fungus makes the soil repel water, so the grass dries out | Break up the soil and get water in. See below |
| A ring of darker green, faster-growing grass | Type 2 | The fungus breaks down organic matter and releases nitrogen | Fertilize the rest of the lawn so it matches |
| A ring of mushrooms after rain, grass looks normal | Type 3 | The fungus is fruiting. It isn't hurting the grass | Pick the mushrooms or leave them alone |

A ring can move between types as the weather changes. A green ring in spring can turn brown in a dry August.

### Type 1: the dead ring

This is the only type that does real damage. A dense mat of mycelium makes the soil shed water like wax, so the grass above it dies of thirst even after you water.

What helps:

1. **Spike or core-aerate** the ring and a foot or so on either side. You want holes all the way through the mat.
2. **Use a wetting agent.** Both the RHS and Wisconsin mention these. They help water soak into the water-repellent soil.
3. **Water deeply** right after, so the water actually reaches the roots.
4. **Feed the grass** to help it recover.

Expect to repeat this. The ring is still alive underneath.

### Type 2: the green ring

Nothing is wrong here. The fungus is releasing nitrogen as it breaks down organic matter, and the grass loves it. Wisconsin suggests fertilizing the rest of the lawn lightly so the ring blends in.

### Type 3: mushrooms only

The fungus is fruiting after wet weather. The grass is fine. Wisconsin suggests picking the mushrooms with gloves and throwing them in the trash if they bother you, especially if kids or dogs use the lawn. The RHS points out that picking them won't stop new rings, because spores travel long distances anyway.

## When fairy rings show up where you live

To answer "when should I expect these," we looked at ten years of iNaturalist sightings of *Marasmius oreades* across the US. That's about 11,000 verified sightings, sorted by region and month.

![Heatmap of fairy ring mushroom sightings by month across five US regions](/fairy-ring-mushrooms-by-region.png)

The timing depends on when your region gets warm, wet weather:

- **Pacific Northwest:** A big spike in **October**, which alone has 34% of sightings. There's a smaller flush in May.
- **California:** A **winter** mushroom. December through January is the peak, when the rains come.
- **Mountain West:** An early summer peak in **June** (30%), running through August.
- **Northeast and Midwest:** A long season from **May through October**, peaking in September.
- **Southeast and South:** Spread from May to October, with peaks in **May and September**.

Rings stay in place between flushes, so these windows tell you when to expect yours to show mushrooms again. A ring that fruits well outside your region's window may be a different species, which is worth a closer look before anyone handles it.

## Identifying the fairy ring mushroom

*Marasmius oreades* is small and plain. Look for all of these together:

- **Cap:** About 1 to 5 cm across, tan to reddish brown, with a small bump in the center. It fades as it dries.
- **Gills:** Widely spaced and pale. They are attached to the stem or free of it, but they **don't run down the stem**.
- **Stem:** Thin, tough, and bendy. You can twist it without it snapping.
- **Spore print:** White.
- **Where:** In open grass, in rings or arcs. Not in the woods.

It has an odd trick. Tom Volk at UW-Madison explains that it can dry out completely in hot weather and come back to life when it rains again, thanks to a sugar called trehalose.

### The lookalike that matters

The **sweating mushroom** (*Clitocybe dealbata*, also listed as *Collybia dealbata*) grows in the same lawns and is poisonous. The key difference is the gills. On the sweating mushroom, they **run down the stem**. Missouri's Department of Conservation also describes it as grayer and pinker.

It contains muscarine. The North American Mycological Association says symptoms usually start within 15 to 30 minutes: heavy sweating, drooling, watery eyes, then vomiting and diarrhea. Severe cases can affect breathing and heart rate. If anyone eats a lawn mushroom and feels sick, call Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222 right away.

## Are fairy ring mushrooms edible?

*Marasmius oreades* is a known edible. Foragers like its nutty flavor, and it dries well. But we'd only eat one with two conditions met:

1. **An experienced forager has confirmed it in person.** Photos aren't enough because of the sweating mushroom.
2. **The lawn hasn't been treated.** Tom Volk warns against eating mushrooms from lawns sprayed with pesticides or herbicides, and especially from golf courses. Fungi can concentrate toxins from the soil.

Keep dogs away from all lawn mushrooms. For more on that, see [lawn mushrooms and dogs](/blog/lawn-mushrooms-and-dogs/).

## Can you get rid of a fairy ring for good?

Honestly, not easily.

The **only sure method** is the one the RHS describes: dig out the ring's soil about 30 cm (1 foot) deep, replace it with fresh soil, and reseed or re-turf. For a big ring, that's a lot of digging.

**Fungicides usually don't work.** The RHS says none are available to home gardeners in the UK for fairy rings. Wisconsin Extension says products labeled for them are typically not effective. They also harm the helpful fungi in your soil.

What does work is managing it:

- **Core-aerate the whole lawn regularly.** Wisconsin notes this cuts the thatch that fairy ring fungi feed on.
- **Water deeply in dry spells**, especially over brown rings.
- **Fertilize evenly** so green rings stand out less.
- **Wait.** Wisconsin says rings often fade on their own when conditions change.

## Fairy ring folklore

The name comes from old European stories. Wisconsin Extension notes the medieval belief that rings appeared where fairies had danced.

In British and Irish folklore, a ring was a place to avoid. Stepping inside could mean being pulled into the fairies' dance and losing track of time. Some tales said you'd come out years later. Others said it brought bad luck. So if you're wondering what happens if you step in a fairy ring, the honest answer is nothing at all. The fungus won't notice you.

Today, gardeners see rings more practically: a sign of buried organic matter and healthy fungal life in the soil.

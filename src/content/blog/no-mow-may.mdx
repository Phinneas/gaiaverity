Every May, the same advice floods social feeds: quit mowing for the month and save the bees. No Mow May started in the UK — conservation charity Plantlife launched it in 2019 — and jumped to the US in 2020, when Appleton, Wisconsin became the first American city to suspend lawn enforcement for the month. Dozens of US cities have followed since.

There's just one problem: the study that anchored the American movement was retracted.

That doesn't mean the whole idea is nonsense — and it doesn't mean the right move is firing up the mower every Saturday like it's 2019. It means the honest answer to "should I stop mowing in May?" comes from better research. And that research points somewhere more interesting: mow *less, all season* instead of *not at all, for one month*. Here's what the evidence supports, what the law says, and how to do right by pollinators without a letter from your HOA.

## The Study That Started It All — and What Went Wrong

Here's the backstory the yard signs leave out.

In September 2020, researchers Israel Del Toro and Relena Ribbons published a study in the journal *PeerJ* reporting that unmowed lawns in Appleton held about three times more bee species than mowed ones. The New York Times covered it. Appleton — where Ribbons taught at Lawrence University — became the first US city to adopt No Mow May as official policy. The movement had its proof.

Except it didn't.

In October 2022, Zach Portman, a bee taxonomist with the University of Minnesota's Bee Lab, published a detailed critique of the paper. Among the problems he documented: the dataset included bee species that don't occur in Wisconsin in May; most specimens were identified by sight in the field, a method that can't reliably distinguish most bee species; and the comparison matched unmowed *home lawns* — often near cultivated flower beds — against mowed *park spaces*, so mowing was never isolated as the variable.

*PeerJ* retracted the paper on November 18, 2022, with a notice citing "several potential inconsistencies in data handling and reporting," agreed to by the authors and the editorial team. The New York Times updated its original story. Appleton's council began reconsidering the policy in 2023, and by 2024 neighboring Fox Crossing had rescinded its No Mow May outright.

This isn't one skeptic's crusade, either — the Xerces Society, the country's leading pollinator-conservation nonprofit, has its own cautions about No Mow May as commonly practiced.

To be clear about what this does and doesn't mean: it does *not* mean mowing less is bad for pollinators. It means the movement's flagship study couldn't support its headline claim. The honest case for mowing less has to come from research that survived scrutiny.

**That research exists.**

## What Actually Works: The Research That Held Up

In 2018, a research team led by USDA forest ecologist Susannah Lerman ran a much tighter experiment, published in *Biological Conservation*. Sixteen suburban yards in Springfield, Massachusetts, followed for two summers, all herbicide-free, each assigned a mowing regime — weekly, every two weeks, or every three weeks. And unlike the retracted study, lawns were compared only with other lawns.

The results:

| Mowing Frequency | Bee Abundance | Bee Diversity | Floral Resources | Grass Height (measured) | Best For |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Weekly** | Low | Low | Baseline | ≈11.2 cm (4.4") | Traditional look; lowest pollinator value |
| **Every 2 Weeks** | **Highest** | Moderate | Baseline | ≈11.2 cm (4.4") | Most bees overall — the practical sweet spot |
| **Every 3 Weeks** | Moderate | **Highest** | **2.5× baseline** | ≈12.5 cm (4.9") | Most species + flowers; fewer mowing sessions |

Two headlines. Yards mowed every two weeks attracted the most bees overall. Yards mowed every three weeks hosted the most bee *species* and roughly 2.5 times more lawn flowers than the more frequently mowed yards. Weekly mowing lost on every count.

Why we trust this one: mowing was the isolated variable, the design was controlled, the journal is solid, and the paper remains intact even as the surrounding literature has been picked apart.

The same research group also tested the most common objection to mowing less — that taller grass means more ticks. They found no significant difference in tick abundance across the weekly, two-week, and three-week regimes. The Lyme-disease argument against longer grass is weaker than people assume.

A 2025 UK study points the same direction: Morrison and colleagues, publishing in the *Conservation Evidence Journal*, found lawns mown at extended intervals of 6–12 weeks supported >170% higher pollinator abundance than standard two-week mowing. Caveat in the same breath: UK lawns and climate aren't US lawns and climate — treat it as directional support for "longer intervals help," not a prescription.

## Slow Mow Summer: The Evidence-Based Alternative

If every-two-weeks mowing is the sweet spot, why does the movement still center on not mowing at all for one month?

Appleton asked itself the same question. After the retraction, the city that started American No Mow May pivoted, promoting a "Slow Mow Summer" instead. The idea is simple: keep mowing, just less often — every two to three weeks, deck set around four inches — and do it *all summer*, not just in May.

Compare the two honestly.

**No Mow May's evidence problem:** the flagship study is retracted, and Xerces points out that the benefits largely evaporate if you resume weekly mowing in June anyway. One restrained month followed by business as usual is a gesture.

**Slow Mow's evidence:** Lerman's intact research puts the abundance peak at two-week intervals and the diversity peak at three — sustained across the season. Follow-up work by Lerman also found mowing every three weeks cuts mowing emissions by roughly a third versus weekly mowing. That's a measurable environmental gain, not a symbolic one.

The legal problem mostly solves itself, too. Under a slow-mow schedule, grass stays in the 4-to-6-inch range all season — nowhere near the 8-to-12-inch thresholds most ordinances enforce (more on that below).

And to be fair to No Mow May: where local rules allow it, a month of unmowed lawn isn't harmful. The case against it is weak evidence plus legal and practical risk — not danger. If your city permits it and you enjoy it, fine. For most readers, though, Slow Mow Summer is the version that survives contact with your city code, your HOA, and the actual science.

> **★ Slow Mow Summer in 5 Steps**
> 1. Set your mower deck to 4 inches.
> 2. Mow when grass reaches about 6 inches — roughly every 2–3 weeks.
> 3. Leave the clippings where they fall.
> 4. Let low flowers (clover, violets, self-heal) bloom between cuts.
> 5. Skip the herbicides — the flowers are the point.

## Before You Stop Mowing: The Ordinance Reality Check

There's no federal lawn law in the United States. What's legal is decided city by city — and if you live under an HOA, enforced twice.

**What "overgrown" means legally.** Most municipal codes define it by height: grass exceeding roughly 8–12 inches violates the ordinance, with fines typically running $25–$500 per violation — and in some jurisdictions, re-issued daily until you comply.

**When enforcement escalates.** These outcomes are rare, but they're real, and both started with an ignored notice:

Jim Ficken of Dunedin, Florida, left town in 2018 to settle his mother's estate. The grass kept growing, and code-enforcement fines began accruing daily — eventually toward $30,000. The Institute for Justice took his case; after losses in district court and the Eleventh Circuit, the city settled for roughly $10,000. To be clear: Ficken's case had nothing to do with No Mow May. It's simply the clearest public example of what ordinary lawn-ordinance enforcement becomes when a notice goes unanswered.

And in Grand Prairie, Texas, Rick Yoes was jailed in 2015 — not for tall grass, but for contempt after ignoring mowing citations for some twenty years and refusing to pay the resulting $1,700 fine.

The lesson from both isn't "you'll go to jail over your lawn." It's that ignoring a code notice is the one move that turns a $50 problem into a five-figure one.

**If you have an HOA, you answer to two rulebooks.** City ordinance and your CC&Rs both apply, and the HOA is often stricter — your city might allow 12 inches while your CC&Rs cap grass at 6. You must satisfy whichever standard is tighter. HOA rules aren't government law; they're a private contract you signed at purchase, and "the city allows it" is not a defense. HOA escalation runs warnings → fines → liens and, in extreme cases, foreclosure.

**The legal path that's emerging: "managed natural landscape" exemptions.** A growing number of states and cities exempt intentional, maintained native plantings from grass-height rules — typically requiring that the landscape be planned rather than neglected, free of noxious weeds, and sometimes registered with the city. Adoption is patchy. This is a call-your-city situation, not an assumption.

**Action steps:**

1. Look up your city or county grass/weed ordinance — city website, or call city hall.
2. If you have an HOA, read your CC&Rs for grass height and mowing-frequency rules. Comply with the stricter of the two.
3. Planning a lawn-to-meadow transition? Ask about a managed natural landscape exemption *before* you stop mowing, not after you're cited.
4. Document intent: file a plan, keep photos, plant receipts, and maintenance records.

**PNW readers:** Washington and Oregon cities tend to be more permissive on lawn codes than Midwest and Southern cities — but an HOA can be strict anywhere. Check both before you commit.

*Ordinances change and vary widely — verify your current local code. This isn't legal advice.*

## If You Get a Violation Notice

Most enforcement follows the same script:

1. **A warning arrives first** — usually a posted notice or a letter. This is the cheap-fix point.
2. **A compliance window opens** — commonly a few weeks.
3. **Fines begin** — in some cities, re-issued daily until compliance.
4. **Escalation** — unpaid fines can become a lien on the property, and some cities will simply mow the lawn themselves and bill you.

What to do, in order:

- **Respond immediately.** Never ignore a notice — both worst-case stories above started with one going unanswered.
- **Document everything.** Photos of plantings, receipts for seed and plants, a simple log of maintenance — weeding, deadheading, mowing dates. If your yard is an intentional landscape, paperwork is what proves it.
- **Ask about exemptions.** If you're cited for a planned natural landscape, raise the managed-natural-landscape question before any hearing, not at it.
- **For HOA violations:** read your CC&Rs, then appeal to the board. "Intentional and maintained" is your argument; your records are your evidence.

## FAQs

**What's the actual downside of No Mow May?**
Legal risk, mainly. Where city codes or HOA rules are enforced, a full month of unmowed grass can climb toward — or past — the height that triggers fines. And the ecological evidence for the "one month unmowed" approach specifically was retracted. Slow Mow Summer delivers the pollinator benefit without the citation risk.

**Does not mowing really increase ticks?**
No. When Lerman's team tested it, they found no significant difference in tick abundance across weekly, two-week, and three-week mowing regimes. The "long grass means Lyme disease" worry doesn't hold up as an argument against mowing less.

**Do I have to skip the entire month of May?**
No — and May isn't actually special. Mow normally through April, then shift to every 2–3 weeks starting in May and keep it going all summer. Same benefit, less risk.

**Why mow less at all, if the study was retracted?**
Because the retracted study was never the only evidence. Lerman's intact research shows real pollinator gains at 2–3 week intervals — two-week yards attracted the most bees, three-week yards the most species. May is symbolic, not magic. The benefit comes from mowing less, year-round.

**Will my neighbors hate me if I let my lawn grow?**
Depends on your locale and your HOA — which is exactly why Slow Mow beats No Mow for most people. Grass stays short enough to read as "maintained," while you're still cutting emissions and feeding pollinators. And the clover's prettier than the dandelions anyway.

## The Bottom Line

The study that launched American No Mow May couldn't survive scrutiny. The research that has survived says something more useful anyway: you don't have to choose between a lawn and pollinators. You just have to mow less often — every two to three weeks, deck at four inches, clippings down, no herbicides. That's the whole playbook, and it works in May and every month after.

Check your city code and your CC&Rs first. Then let the flowers bloom between cuts — and keep your sanity *and* your clean code record at the same time.

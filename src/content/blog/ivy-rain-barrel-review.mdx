## What Works, What Doesn't

### The good

Everything you need for assembly ships in the box: barrel, cap, lid, pre-installed screen and ring, flex overflow hose, ball valve spigot, reusable zip ties, linking hose. You supply blocks, a hacksaw, tape measure, marker, gloves and safety glasses. No hunting for a matching fitting.

The access port sits at the lowest point on the barrel, so it fully drains. That matters more than it sounds. Standing water is where smell and mosquito problems start.

The locking lid with reusable zip ties and the pre-installed mosquito screen handle the two most common rain barrel complaints: kids and pets getting in, and insects breeding. The screen comes installed from the factory.

Linking capability and two overflow ports let you chain barrels and route excess water away from the foundation. Three Ivys fit in the back seat of a mid-size sedan, so picking one up from a municipal sale is realistic.

### The bad

Elevation is on you. If you raise the barrel, the manufacturer recommends a solid, level foundation with a 24"W x 24"L base, and warns the full barrel weighs over 420 pounds. Skip the base and you risk a tipped barrel.

Winter is a chore. Drain Ivy before temperatures drop below freezing, and note the warranty excludes damage from failure to winterize. The manufacturer also says 50 gallons gives about eight minutes of watering, so a pump (sold separately, moving eight plus gallons per minute) is the practical answer for real irrigation.

For gravity-fed drip or soaker hoses, my honest read is that the elevation most people build isn't enough. A pump is the fix, not taller blocks.

## Practical Questions Before You Buy

### Can I drink the water from an Ivy rain barrel?

No. Rain Water Solutions doesn't recommend consuming water from its rain barrel or any rain barrel until the water is thoroughly tested, and the company notes that most homes have asphalt shingles that render the water undrinkable unless filtered and cleaned appropriately. That is a manufacturer talking about its own product, which tells you how seriously to take it. If you wanted to make the water drinkable you would need a filtration system, and the company still recommends testing the water thoroughly. There is no filter you can bolt onto a barrel and forget about. I would treat every rain barrel, including this one, as garden water only.

### Will the Ivy run a drip line or soaker hose?

Only with help. Gravity gives you very little pressure at a spigot that sits near the ground, so a soaker hose will barely weep. The practical answer is a transfer pump, which home improvement stores sell and which requires an electrical outlet. Rain Water Solutions says these pumps work well and can move eight plus gallons of water per minute. Since Ivy holds 50 gallons, that works out to about eight minutes of watering. That short window is the real argument for a pump: it lets you empty the barrel fast when rain is in the forecast and you want to make room for the next storm. Don't leave the pump running once Ivy is empty.

## FAQ

### In what states are rain barrels illegal?

No state bans rain barrels outright. The restrictions that exist are local or regional, and they usually target how much water you store or where it goes, not the barrel itself. Colorado once limited residential collection, and it has since loosened those rules. If you live in a western state where water rights are tightly allocated, check with your county or municipal water authority before you buy. Rain Water Solutions, which makes the Ivy, sells into municipal and county programs across the country, so the barrel itself isn't the issue. The permit is.

### Who makes the best rain barrel?

There is no single best maker, because the right barrel depends on your downspout, your budget and whether you want a subsidy. What I can say is that the Ivy is unusually well documented. Rain Water Solutions publishes its dimensions, its empty and full weights, a maintenance sheet and a warranty, and the barrel is sold through city and county programs in North Carolina, including Durham and Buncombe County. That transparency is worth more than a brand name. My pick for a first barrel is whichever one your local water authority subsidizes, since the same barrel can cost far less through a program.

### What are the downsides of using a rain barrel?

Weight is the main one. Rain Water Solutions states that "When full, your 50 gallon rain barrel will weigh 420 lbs," and the company's own spec sheet lists a full Ivy at 424 pounds. That's a lot of mass sitting against your foundation, so a level, solid base matters. Capacity is the next catch. The same source notes that Ivy holds 50 gallons, which it describes as "about eight minutes of watering" if you run a transfer pump. You'll empty it faster than you expect. Then there's winter: you have to drain it before freezing weather and keep the screen clear.

### How long can rainwater sit in a barrel?

There's no fixed shelf life, and the sources don't give one. Light, temperature and how much organic matter washes in off your roof determine it. The Ivy is made of opaque HDPE, which blocks light and slows algal growth, and it ships with a mosquito screen and a locking lid. Those features buy you time, not immunity. In a hot climate I would use the water within a few weeks and refill often. In a cool one it keeps longer. If the water smells or looks cloudy, drain it and start over rather than pouring it on edible crops.

### Rain Barrel with Air Access-any suggestion on what I can add to the collected rain water that will help alleviate insect breeding but not kill plants?

The screen is the answer, not an additive. The Ivy ships with a mosquito screen and ring pre-installed from the factory, and Rain Water Solutions lists the screen, the locking lid and the overflow hose as included parts. Mosquitoes need open water to breed, so a sealed lid plus an intact screen solves the problem without putting anything in the water. If your screen tears, the company suggests cutting a replacement using the old screen as a template. I would skip mosquito dunks and oils. They aren't needed when the barrel is properly screened, and you avoid any question about what they do to your soil.
